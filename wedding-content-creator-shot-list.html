A week or two before your wedding, your content creator will send a questionnaire that asks, in some form, “send us your shot list and any inspiration.” Most couples freeze — you have never planned vertical video before, and a photographer’s list is the wrong document to copy. This is the short version: which candid clips matter, in order of the day, and exactly what to put in the brief.

Use the list below as a menu, not a script. Tick the clips you genuinely care about, ignore the rest, and leave your creator room to catch what cannot be planned — those unplanned reactions are the footage couples rewatch for years. When you are ready, the one-page printable checklist is at the end.

The one rule: a content shot list is about movement and reaction, shot vertically and candidly. It is not your photographer’s posed family-formal list. Send each vendor their own document so nobody shows up expecting the wrong thing.

The shot list, by part of the day

Every wedding differs, so treat these as the moments couples most often say they are grateful to have on camera. The creator keys off your timeline, not the clock, so approximate windows are fine. Bold items are the “cannot-miss” core most couples keep even on a short package.

  1. Getting ready & detailsThe dress or suit going on, hands fastening jewelry and cufflinks, the invitation and rings in close-up, toasts with the bridal party, nerves and laughter in hair and makeup, and a quiet first look with a parent or bridesmaid. This is the calmest window and the easiest to over-film — a handful of genuine moments beats a long montage.
  2. First look & portraitsThe partner’s genuine reaction on first seeing you, a walk hand-in-hand, the veil or train moving in the wind, and wide establishing shots of the venue. Keep staged trends here, out of the ceremony, so they never interrupt the real thing.
  3. CeremonyThe walk down the aisle, partner and parent reactions during vows, the ring exchange, and the first kiss — plus guests dabbing eyes, the officiant’s lighter line, and your joined hands. Coordinate positions so the creator never blocks your photographer; the formal first-kiss angle belongs to the pro.
  4. Cocktail hourThe two of you sneaking away to breathe, guests laughing and hugging, signature drinks and decor in close-up, and reactions to the room reveal. This is pure behind-the-scenes texture your formal gallery often skips.
  5. Grand entrance & toastsThe wedding party and your entrance, and each full speech with the speaker and your reactions both filmed — laughs, tears, raised glasses. Audio matters here; confirm the creator mics up or positions near the speaker.
  6. First dance & parent dancesThe full first dance from a couple of angles, the parent dances, guests gathering around the floor, and the cake cutting. Dim light is the risk, so this is where a creator with a gimbal and small light earns the fee.
  7. Party & send-offDance-floor energy, the one guest who goes all in, the bouquet toss if you do it, golden-hour or night exterior shots, and the sparkler or getaway exit. Share the send-off time the moment you know it — it is the most commonly missed shot of the night.

Name the people, not just the moments. “My grandmother, my sister’s toast, my husband opening my gift” is far more useful than “candids of guests.” List roughly five VIPs and the one or two moments involving each that you would hate to lose.

Content creator list vs. photographer list

The confusion starts because both documents are called a “shot list.” They are different deliverables made by different people, and handing your creator a photographer’s list (or vice versa) is the most common mistake couples make.

FormatVertical video, movement and sound, candidPosed, high-resolution stills
Core itemsReactions, hugs, speeches, dance floor, trendsFamily formals, named groupings, portraits
Who directsMostly observes, occasionally sets a trendActively poses and arranges groups

Give the photographer the full family-formal lineup with first names. Give the content creator your timeline, the handful of reactions and VIPs above, and the vibe you want. More on how the two work together without getting in each other’s way is in the questions to ask before you book.

The 5-part brief to actually send

You do not need to write an essay. Creators tell us the most useful pre-wedding brief fits on a page and answers five questions. Send it with your timeline attached and links to two or three saved videos you love.

  • Your timeline. Order of the day with rough times, the venue layout, and where each key moment happens. Mark anything still unconfirmed.
  • 5–10 must-have clips. The moments above you refuse to lose. Keep it short so they stay a safety net, not a 50-shot script.
  • Your VIPs. A few named people and the one moment each matters for — a parent, a sibling, a terminally ill relative, a childhood friend.
  • The do-not-film rules. Anyone who must stay off camera (children, privacy requests), anything you do not want posted live, and whether you approve before anything goes public.
  • Style & trends. Trend-forward or timeless; two or three saved links; any music you love or hate; whether you want real-time Stories posted or everything sent privately first.

Mistakes that quietly ruin the footage

  • Sending fifty dictated shots. The creator spends the day ticking boxes and misses the candid reactions that make the best reels.
  • Describing a trend from memory. Send the saved link; “that one where we turn around” means ten different things.
  • Forgetting the send-off time. Getaway exits happen once and are the #1 missed shot when the timeline changed and nobody told the creator.
  • No do-not-film list. A two-line privacy note prevents an awkward takedown request involving a guest later.
  • Not flagging audio moments. Vows and toasts need a mic plan or a quiet room; a bare phone in a loud hall is unrecoverable.
  • Giving both vendors the same list. Posed formals and candid clips are different jobs — separate documents, shared timeline.

Frequently asked questions

Do I really need to give my wedding content creator a shot list?

Yes, but it does not have to be long. A good creator can film an entire day on instinct, yet they cannot know which five moments matter most to you, which guests are non-negotiable, or that a trending transition you love exists. A one-page brief with your timeline, a handful of must-have clips, your VIP names, and any do-not-film rules is what turns generic coverage into footage you will actually rewatch. Creators routinely ask for exactly this in their pre-wedding questionnaire.

What is the difference between a content creator shot list and a photographer shot list?

A photographer’s list is mostly posed, high-resolution stills and named family groupings that the photographer directs on the day. A content creator shot list is vertical, movement-driven behind-the-scenes footage: reactions, hugs, speeches, the dance floor, and short trends. You do not send a content creator your family-formal lineup; you send your timeline, the reactions and people you care about, and the vibe you want. The photographer owns the key formal angles and the creator works around them.

How long should a wedding content creator shot list be?

One page is ideal: five to ten must-have clips, your timeline, named VIPs, privacy rules, and two or three trend ideas at most. A list of fifty dictated shots works against you, because the creator spends the day checking boxes instead of catching the unplanned moments that make the best reels. Treat it as a safety net for what cannot be missed, not a shot-by-shot script.

When do I send the shot list, and what if my timeline is not final?

Send a first version when your creator sends their pre-wedding questionnaire, usually two to four weeks before the wedding, and confirm the final timeline about a week out once your planner and venue have locked the schedule. If times are still moving, send approximate windows and mark the moments that will not change, such as the first look, vows, speeches, first dance, and send-off. Precise minutes matter less than the order of the day.

Will a detailed shot list make my wedding footage feel stiff or over-directed?

Not if you limit the list to must-have moments and leave everything else open. The point is to protect a handful of clips you would regret losing, then give a professional room to capture candid reactions. Over-directing is a real risk: asking the creator to stage dozens of trends can interrupt the day and put them in the photographer’s way. Name the few trends you love in advance so they can be planned around the timeline, then let the rest happen.

Should I list specific TikTok or Instagram Reels trends?

Yes, two or three is helpful, sent as saved links rather than described from memory. Tell the creator whether you want trend-forward edits or a timeless, story-driven reel, because those are different editing styles. An experienced creator will tell you honestly which trends fit your timeline, what props or people they need, and which ones would force you to repeat a genuine moment for the camera.

Can a friend with a phone use this list instead of hiring a creator?

For a small or low-budget wedding, yes: a trusted friend armed with this list, a charged phone, and clear audio expectations can capture usable getting-ready and reception clips. The gap shows up in consistency, low light, audio during vows and speeches, and coordination with your professional vendors. A hired creator brings backup gear, stabilization, microphones, fast delivery, and the discipline to keep filming while your friend enjoys the day. Use the list either way, but know what you are trading.

My photographer says they already capture these moments. Do I still need the list?

There is real overlap, which is worth discussing before you book anyone, but the deliverables differ: photographers prioritize composed, archival stills and usually deliver a gallery weeks later, while a content creator captures vertical candid video and reactions within a day or two. Even if you only have a photographer, a short list of the candid clips you most want does not hurt. If you have both, give each vendor their own list and make sure the creator has agreed to stay out of the photographer’s key shots.
